Albert Einstein was born on March 14, 1879, in Ulm, in the German Empire,
and grew up mostly in Munich. As a child he was curious about the natural
world from an early age — a family story often told is that a pocket
compass given to him around age five left a deep impression, sparking his
fascination with invisible forces like magnetism. He was not an early
academic star in every subject, but excelled in mathematics and physics
from his teenage years onward.

He studied physics and mathematics at the Swiss Federal Polytechnic in
Zurich, graduating in 1900. Unable to secure an academic post immediately
after graduating, he took a position as a technical examiner at the Swiss
patent office in Bern starting in 1902. It was during this period, often
called his "miracle year," that in 1905 he published four groundbreaking
papers: one explaining the photoelectric effect (using the idea that light
behaves as discrete packets of energy, later called photons), one on
Brownian motion providing strong evidence for the existence of atoms, one
introducing the special theory of relativity, and one deriving the
equivalence of mass and energy, expressed in the equation E = mc^2.

Special relativity overturned the classical idea of absolute space and
time, showing that measurements of time and length depend on the observer's
motion. Einstein built on this over the following decade, and in 1915 he
published the general theory of relativity, which described gravity not as
a force acting across space but as the curvature of space and time itself
caused by mass and energy. This theory was dramatically confirmed in 1919
when astronomers observed starlight bending around the sun during a solar
eclipse, exactly as his equations predicted — an event that made Einstein
world-famous almost overnight.

In 1921 he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, officially for his
explanation of the photoelectric effect rather than relativity, since
relativity was still considered too new and unproven by some physicists at
the time to be the basis for the prize.

Einstein was deeply skeptical of the probabilistic interpretation of
quantum mechanics that emerged in the late 1920s, famously resistant to the
idea that nature's fundamental laws could be based on chance. This is
often summarized by his remark that he could not believe God plays dice
with the universe — reflecting his belief that a deeper, deterministic
explanation must underlie quantum phenomena, a position that put him at
odds with contemporaries like Niels Bohr.

He held that imagination and intuition were at least as important to
scientific discovery as accumulated knowledge, often describing his own
breakthroughs as beginning with visual thought experiments — such as
imagining what it would be like to travel alongside a beam of light —
rather than starting from formal mathematics, which he would only bring in
afterward to make the intuition rigorous.

As a Jewish scientist in Germany, Einstein emigrated to the United States
in 1933 after the Nazi party came to power, taking a position at the newly
formed Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey, where he
remained for the rest of his life. In 1939, concerned that Nazi Germany
might develop nuclear weapons, he co-signed a letter to President
Roosevelt urging the U.S. to begin nuclear research — a decision he later
said he regretted, given the eventual use of atomic bombs on Hiroshima and
Nagasaki. In his later years he became an outspoken advocate for nuclear
disarmament, world government as a path to lasting peace, and civil rights
in the United States, corresponding and publicly aligning himself with
activists such as W. E. B. Du Bois.

Einstein was also known for his personal habits and philosophy: he valued
simplicity, reportedly owned few pairs of the same style of clothing to
avoid decision fatigue, played the violin as a lifelong hobby, and
maintained a certain playful humility about fame, often redirecting
attention back to the ideas rather than himself. He continued working,
including an unsuccessful pursuit of a "unified field theory" attempting to
merge gravity and electromagnetism, until his death on April 18, 1955, in
Princeton.

He was offered the presidency of Israel in 1952 following the death of
Chaim Weizmann, but declined, stating he lacked the aptitude and experience
for statesmanship, though he remained a committed supporter of Jewish
cultural and scientific institutions throughout his life, including helping
found the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.
