Avul Pakir Jainulabdeen Abdul Kalam was born on October 15, 1931, in
Rameswaram, a small pilgrim town in the Madras Presidency of British
India, into a Tamil Muslim family of modest means. His father owned a
boat used to ferry pilgrims, and young Kalam sold newspapers as a boy to
supplement the family's income and pay for his schooling. He often
described his upbringing as one shaped by discipline, close family bonds,
and the values of hard work instilled by his parents.

He studied physics at St. Joseph's College, Tiruchirappalli, and then
pursued aerospace engineering at the Madras Institute of Technology,
graduating in 1960. He joined the Aeronautical Development Establishment
of the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) and later
moved to the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), where he served
as project director for India's first indigenous Satellite Launch Vehicle,
SLV-III, which successfully placed the Rohini satellite into orbit in
1980.

Kalam returned to DRDO in the 1980s and led the Integrated Guided Missile
Development Programme, overseeing the development of missiles including
Agni and Prithvi. This work, combined with his role in India's 1998
Pokhran-II nuclear tests as Chief Scientific Adviser to the Prime Minister,
earned him widespread recognition as one of the architects of India's
missile and nuclear capability, and he became popularly known as the
"Missile Man of India."

In 2002, Kalam was elected the 11th President of India, serving until
2007. He was notable for being seen as a president above narrow political
affiliation, widely respected across party lines, and became known
especially for his engagement with young people — visiting schools and
colleges throughout his presidency and after, encouraging students to
dream big and pursue science and innovation for national development.

Kalam consistently emphasized that dreams must be paired with hard work
and discipline to become reality. He spoke often about setting a clear
vision for India's development, notably promoting the idea of "India
2020," a vision of India as a developed nation achieved through progress
in agriculture, infrastructure, education, healthcare, information
technology, and self-reliance in critical technologies.

He wrote several books, including his autobiography "Wings of Fire" and
"Ignited Minds," in which he explored themes of ambition, national
development, and the potential of India's youth. He believed strongly
that small towns and modest backgrounds were no barrier to achievement,
often citing his own journey from Rameswaram as an example, and he
encouraged students to value curiosity, perseverance, and moral
character alongside technical skill.

Despite his central role in defence and missile technology, Kalam was
known for a simple, disciplined personal lifestyle — he never married,
lived modestly, and was often described by those who knew him as deeply
spiritual, reading religious texts of multiple faiths and valuing
harmony between science and spirituality rather than seeing them as
opposed.

After his presidency, he continued teaching at institutions including the
Indian Institute of Management Shillong, IIT Delhi, and IIM Ahmedabad,
remaining highly active in public life and youth outreach. He died on
July 27, 2015, while delivering a lecture to students at the Indian
Institute of Management Shillong — a manner of death many saw as
fitting for someone who spent much of his later life engaging directly
with young learners.
