Nikola Tesla was born on July 10, 1856, in the village of Smiljan, in what
was then the Austrian Empire (present-day Croatia), to a Serbian family —
his father was an Orthodox priest and his mother, though uneducated, was
known for inventing small household mechanical devices, which Tesla later
credited as an early influence on his own inventive instincts. He showed
an unusual memory and vivid visual imagination from childhood, later
describing an ability to picture inventions in complete, precise detail in
his mind before ever building them, testing and refining designs mentally
before touching any physical materials.

He studied engineering at the Austrian Polytechnic in Graz and later
attended the University of Prague, though he did not complete a formal
degree. He worked briefly for a telephone company in Budapest, where he
reportedly conceived the basic principle of the rotating magnetic field —
later the foundation of his alternating current (AC) induction motor —
while walking in a park and visualizing the concept fully formed.

In 1884, Tesla emigrated to the United States and briefly worked for
Thomas Edison's company, but the two disagreed sharply, partly over unpaid
promised compensation and partly over a deeper technical dispute: Edison
championed direct current (DC) electrical systems, while Tesla firmly
believed alternating current was superior for transmitting electricity
efficiently over long distances. This disagreement escalated into the
well-known "War of the Currents" during the late 1880s and early 1890s,
during which Edison's side ran public campaigns to portray AC as dangerous.
Tesla, backed by industrialist George Westinghouse, ultimately saw AC
become the dominant standard for electrical power distribution worldwide,
a system still in use today.

Tesla held over 300 patents across his lifetime and worked on an
extraordinarily wide range of ideas: the AC induction motor and
polyphase power system, the Tesla coil (a high-voltage transformer circuit
still used in radio technology and demonstrations today), early work on
wireless transmission of information and power, X-ray experimentation, and
remote-controlled devices — he publicly demonstrated a radio-controlled
boat in 1898, decades ahead of when such technology became common.

He was known for grand, sometimes unrealized ambitions. His Wardenclyffe
Tower project, begun in 1901 with funding partly from financier J.P.
Morgan, aimed to create a system for wireless transmission of both
information and electrical power across the globe. The project ran out of
funding before completion and was eventually demolished, marking one of
the most significant setbacks of his career and contributing to his
declining fortunes in later decades.

Tesla was famous for his showmanship as well as his engineering — he gave
dramatic public demonstrations involving high-voltage electricity passing
harmlessly through his own body to illustrate principles of AC current,
and he was known to make bold predictions about the future, including
ideas resembling wireless communication devices, remote sensing, and
global information networks, decades before such technologies existed in
practical form.

Personally, Tesla was known for an intensely disciplined and somewhat
eccentric lifestyle: he never married, believing romantic relationships
would interfere with his scientific work, kept unusual and precise daily
routines, and in his later years developed a well-documented fondness for
feeding and caring for pigeons in New York City, which he described as a
source of comfort. He held strong opinions about the importance of
imagination combined with rigorous testing, often stating that an idea
must be worked out completely in the mind before being built, to avoid
wasting resources on flawed designs.

Despite his major contributions, Tesla died in relative obscurity and
financial difficulty on January 7, 1943, in a New York hotel room. His
reputation and recognition grew substantially in the decades after his
death, and he is now widely regarded as one of the most important
inventors and electrical engineers in history, with the SI unit of magnetic
flux density (the tesla) named in his honor.
